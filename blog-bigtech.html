Working at Big Tech: The Uber Experience

September 2025 • 10 min read

Career Tech

I joined Uber with stars in my eyes and imposter syndrome in my heart. Coming from a startup that barely had a real office, walking into the San Francisco headquarters felt like entering a different dimension. Chrome and glass everywhere. Free food that was actually good. Engineers casually discussing systems that served hundreds of millions of users.

Three years later, I left to start my own company. Here's what I learned about working at one of the world's most ambitious tech companies.

The Scale Will Blow Your Mind

Scale changes everything. At Uber, a single line of code can impact millions of riders and drivers globally. The infrastructure, the tooling, the sheer engineering excellence— it's unmatched. You learn to think in systems, not features.

My first project was optimizing a caching layer. Sounds boring, right? Except this cache handled 2 million requests per second. A 10ms improvement meant saving tens of thousands of dollars monthly in infrastructure costs. More importantly, it meant riders saw available drivers 10ms faster. Multiply that by millions of requests, and suddenly you're saving people collective years of waiting time.

This is what big tech does to your brain—it recalibrates what "impact" means. At a startup, you celebrate getting to 100 users. At Uber, you're thinking about how your code will behave when it serves the entire population of Tokyo simultaneously.

The tooling is decades ahead of most companies. Deployment pipelines that can safely push code to production dozens of times a day. Observability platforms that let you trace a single request through hundreds of microservices. Load testing frameworks that can simulate millions of concurrent users. This infrastructure doesn't just make you productive—it fundamentally changes how you think about building software.

The People Are Extraordinary

The talent density is incredible. Your code reviews come from people who've built systems at Facebook, Google, Amazon. Every lunch conversation is a masterclass in distributed systems, product thinking, or scaling challenges you've only read about.

I learned more in my first six months at Uber than in the previous two years combined. Not from formal training (though that existed), but from osmosis. Sitting in design reviews where senior engineers casually discussed the tradeoffs between different consensus algorithms. Watching how principal engineers navigated complex technical decisions with incomplete information. Learning how to write design docs that actually get read and acted upon.

The diversity of problems was staggering. The engineer next to me was working on fraud detection systems processing billions of transactions. Across the hall, someone was optimizing route algorithms to save drivers 30 seconds per trip. Another team was building real-time mapping systems that had to work in cities with terrible GPS signals.

You're constantly humbled. Think you're good at backend systems? Meet the engineer who literally wrote the book on distributed databases. Think you understand mobile performance? Talk to the person who shaved 100KB off the app binary and improved startup time by 200ms.

The Compensation Is Life-Changing

Compensation and perks speak for themselves. The financial security lets you focus on craft without distraction. The brand on your resume opens doors everywhere.

Let's be direct about this: the money is incredible. Total comp that would take a decade to accumulate at most startups arrives in your bank account over four years. You can pay off student loans. Buy a house (well, maybe not in SF, but elsewhere). Build a financial cushion that gives you real freedom.

The perks are almost comical. Three meals a day, actually good coffee, unlimited snacks, gym membership, commuter benefits, generous PTO, parental leave that you can actually take, learning budgets, home office stipends. The list goes on. You stop thinking about these things, which is precisely the point—remove all friction so you can focus on work.

But here's what no one tells you: the money changes your risk tolerance. Suddenly, the idea of leaving to join a startup (or start one) feels terrifying. You've adjusted your lifestyle. You have a mortgage. Maybe a family. Those RSUs vesting quarterly are addictive.

The Impact Feels Diluted

But there's a cost. Your impact feels diluted. You're one engineer among thousands, working on a sliver of a product that may or may not see the light of day. Org changes can wipe out months of work overnight.

This was the hardest adjustment for me. At my previous startup, I owned entire features from conception to deployment. At Uber, I was on a team of 12 engineers working on one component of one service in one vertical. My code was important, sure, but I was a cog in a massive machine.

Projects get cancelled all the time. We spent three months building a new real-time pricing model. Ran extensive A/B tests. The data looked promising. Then a reorg happened, priorities shifted, and the project was shelved. All that work—hundreds of engineering hours—just... gone. Not because it was bad, but because company strategy changed.

You learn to detach your ego from your work. That beautiful architecture you designed? It might get deprecated next quarter. That feature you shipped? It might get rolled back due to unexpected side effects. Your manager who championed your project? They moved to a different team, and the new manager has different priorities.

Process Becomes Bureaucracy

Process becomes bureaucracy. What starts as "let's move fast safely" becomes endless reviews, stakeholder alignment, and launch approvals. Shipping something simple takes quarters, not days.

Want to change a button color? Better get design approval, PM approval, accessibility review, legal review (no, seriously), privacy review, localization support, and approval from your skip-level manager. Then schedule the experiment, wait for statistical significance, write the experiment analysis, get approval to launch, coordinate with the release train, and maybe —just maybe—that button color changes three months after you first thought of it.

I'm not saying this is all bad. Most of these processes exist for good reasons. When you're operating at Uber's scale, a bug can cost millions of dollars or strand thousands of people. But the cognitive overhead is real. You spend more time navigating process than writing code.

Meetings multiply like rabbits. Stand-ups, sprint planning, backlog grooming, design reviews, architecture reviews, stakeholder syncs, cross-team syncs, all-hands, skip-levels, one-on-ones, retros. Some weeks, I had barely 15 hours for actual focused work. The rest was meetings and context switching.

The Golden Handcuffs Are Real

The golden handcuffs are real. The comfort becomes a trap. You stop taking risks. You optimize for promotions instead of learning. You forget what it's like to build something from scratch.

Around year two, I noticed something troubling. I had become really good at navigating Uber's internal systems, writing Uber-style design docs, and playing the promotion game. But I wasn't sure I was becoming a better engineer. I was becoming a better Uber engineer, which is not the same thing.

The skills that matter at big tech—stakeholder management, writing persuasive docs, navigating politics—are valuable but they're not engineering. Meanwhile, I'd almost forgotten how to just build something end-to-end. Need a database? There's an internal team for that. Need to deploy? There's a platform for that. Need monitoring? Just use the standard tools.

You become specialized to the point of fragility. I knew Uber's tech stack intimately but couldn't remember the last time I'd set up a server from scratch or made architectural decisions without six layers of approval. I was highly effective within the system, but would I be able to build something outside it?

Why I Left

I didn't leave because Uber was bad. I left because I was getting too comfortable. The learning curve had flattened. I was making great money, had job security, and worked with brilliant people. But I felt the itch—the need to build something of my own, to make decisions without consensus, to move fast and break things (my things, not production).

The decision to leave was terrifying. I was walking away from unvested equity worth six figures. From health insurance. From that comfortable salary. From the prestige of having "Uber" on my LinkedIn. My parents thought I was crazy. Some of my friends did too.

But I had learned what I needed to learn. I understood how to build systems at scale. I had a network of brilliant engineers. I had saved enough to give myself runway. Most importantly, I remembered why I got into tech in the first place—to build things, not to navigate org charts.

The Verdict

Big tech is an incredible training ground. But it's not the endgame—at least not for everyone. If you're early in your career, go work at a big tech company. The learning, the network, the financial cushion—it's all invaluable. You'll level up faster than anywhere else.

But don't stay too long. There's a window, somewhere between 2-5 years, where you've learned the lessons but haven't yet been domesticated by the comfort. That's when you should think hard about what you actually want. If you love the structure, the stability, the focus on optimization at scale, stay and climb the ladder. There's nothing wrong with that path.

But if you feel the itch to build, to own something from end to end, to make decisions without consensus—leave. Start a company, join an early-stage startup, become a consultant. Use what you learned, but don't let the golden handcuffs keep you somewhere you've outgrown.

I don't regret my time at Uber for a second. It made me the engineer I am today. But I also don't regret leaving. The best time to leave a big tech company is when you still remember how to build things from scratch—before the comfort becomes a cage.

Now go build something.
