Applying to Y Combinator and Getting In

October 2025 • 8 min read

The YC application is deceptively simple. Just a few questions, a video, and a dream. But behind that simplicity lies weeks of iteration, soul-searching, and brutal honesty about what you're actually building and why.

The Application That Almost Wasn't

I almost didn't apply. The imposter syndrome was real. We had customers, sure, but was our growth fast enough? Our tech stack wasn't particularly novel. Our team was small. Every doubt you can imagine ran through my head in those final days before the deadline.

What changed my mind was a simple realization: YC isn't looking for perfect companies. They're looking for founders who are obsessed with solving real problems. We had that. Our users loved what we were building. The metrics, while not hockey-stick yet, showed consistent week-over-week growth. Most importantly, we understood our users' pain better than anyone else in the market.

Writing the Application

The key insight: they're not looking for polished pitch decks or impressive credentials. They're looking for founders who deeply understand a problem and are relentlessly focused on solving it. Show traction if you have it. Show clarity if you don't.

Every question in the YC application is designed to cut through the noise. "What is your company going to make?" Don't tell them about your vision for the future of work. Tell them exactly what you're building in one sentence. "Why did you pick this idea?" They want to know if you stumbled into this by accident or if you have genuine insight into the problem space.

The hardest question was "What do you understand about your business that other companies in it just don't get?" This forced us to articulate our unfair advantage. For us, it was our direct experience with the problem. We weren't building a solution based on market research—we were building the tool we desperately needed ourselves.

The Video

Our video was shot in one take, no editing. We talked about the problem like we were explaining it to a friend. That authenticity mattered more than production value.

I've seen people stress for weeks about their YC video. Hiring videographers, writing scripts, doing 50 takes. We did none of that. My co-founder and I sat in front of a laptop camera and just talked. We explained the problem, showed a quick demo, and shared our early traction. Total recording time: 12 minutes. Time spent editing: 0 minutes.

The lack of polish actually worked in our favor. It felt real. You could see our genuine excitement about the problem we were solving. There were awkward pauses, we stumbled over words a few times, but it was human. And that's what YC wants to see—real founders, not actors playing founders.

The Wait

After hitting submit, the waiting began. YC receives thousands of applications each batch. The odds are brutal. We tried not to think about it, but of course, we thought about it constantly. Every email notification made my heart skip. Every time I checked our application status, it still said "pending."

Three weeks later, we got the interview invitation. I read the email three times to make sure I wasn't hallucinating. We had ten minutes to convince some of the smartest investors in the world that we were worth betting on. No pressure.

The Interview

The YC interview is legendary for being intense. Ten minutes with partners who have seen thousands of startups. They'll find the weak points in your thinking faster than you can blink. Our strategy was simple: know our numbers cold, be honest about what we don't know, and show that we can think on our feet.

The questions came rapid-fire. "How did you get your first customers?" "What's your burn rate?" "Why will you win against competitor X?" "What if Google builds this?" We answered as directly as possible. When we didn't know something, we said so, but explained how we'd figure it out.

The whole thing felt like it lasted 60 seconds. In reality, it was probably closer to 12 minutes. They thanked us for our time, and we were escorted out. That was it. No indication of whether it went well or terribly.

Getting In

Getting the acceptance email was surreal. I was in the middle of a customer call when it came through. I had to mute myself to keep from shouting. We were in. YC W26. One of the roughly 250 companies selected from thousands of applications.

The celebration lasted about an hour. Then reality hit. We had three months to make as much progress as possible. YC doesn't make companies successful—it accelerates what's already there. If you're not already moving, YC won't push you. But if you're moving, they'll help you move faster than you thought possible.

Advice for Applicants

If you're considering applying to YC, here's my honest advice: Just apply. Don't wait until you have more traction. Don't wait until your product is more polished. Don't wait until you feel "ready." If you're solving a real problem and making any progress at all, apply.

Be specific in your answers. Generalities are the enemy. "We're building AI for healthcare" tells them nothing. "We're using computer vision to detect diabetic retinopathy from smartphone photos, reducing diagnosis time from weeks to seconds" tells them everything.

Show, don't tell. You say you have product-market fit? Show them your retention curves. You say users love your product? Show them the messages users send you. Data beats claims every time.

Most importantly, be yourself. They've seen every type of founder imaginable. They can smell bullshit from a mile away. If you're technical and awkward, be technical and awkward. If you're a smooth talker, be a smooth talker. The worst thing you can do is pretend to be someone you're not.

YC isn't the only path to success. Plenty of great companies never went through YC. But if you're an early-stage founder looking for the absolute best crash course in building a startup, there's nowhere better. The advice, the network, the accountability—it's worth every bit of equity you give up.

Now get back to building. That's what actually matters.
